OVERVIEW

3.41 Seconds Faster

Working with a team of two designers, we redesigned the Steam mobile app to address confusing navigation caused by its desktop-first information architecture. By building upon the existing interface with a familiar bottom navigation bar for everyday actions, we made frequently used features easier to access.

Our redesign reduced average task completion time by 3.21 seconds, helping users find their friends and games more efficiently.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

Leveling Up Steam Mobile

The Steam mobile app suffers from a significant disconnect between its information architecture and standard mobile user expectations. It struggles to balance being a storefront, a social platform, and a news hub, resulting in a "guessing game" for users. High-value features are often buried under vague labels like "Community," leading to low discoverability and high cognitive load.

RESEARCH & PAIN POINTS

Finding the Bugs

Through usability testing with 4 participants, we identified recurring navigation issues that prevented users from moving through the app confidently. Participants frequently relied on guesswork rather than in-app affordances.

  1. 1. Low Discoverability
    Key Finding: 3 out of 4 participants searched the hamburger menu for Friends, while 2 out of 4 expected Family Management to be located there
    • Mismatch between users' mental models and Steam's information architecture
    • Caused repeated navigation errors and interrupted task flow
    Original Navigation Layout

    Users defaulted to searching top navigation bars and hamburger icons to find core social features, ignoring the obscure icon shortcuts

  2. 2. Poor Information Breadcrumbs
    Key Finding: 2 out of 4 participants followed the Community tab expecting it to lead directly to Friends or Family Management
    • Category labels failed to communicate destination content accurately
    • Created misleading paths that increased user friction and task times
  3. 3. Breaking Platform Standards
    Key Finding: Search filters were embedded inside the search field rather than alongside it, forcing unexpected interaction patterns
    • Participants struggled to locate basic filtering capabilities
    • Unclear interaction feedback left users uncertain if pressing "Apply" was required
  4. 4. High Cognitive Load
    Key Finding: New users described the primary screens as overwhelming due to dense, unorganized information displays and split menus
    • Missing visual cues and unclear affordances made UI elements hard to interpret
    • Inconsistent element placement hindered visual scanning
    Original Menus

    Splitting features across two separate menus caused visual clutter and confusion over where settings lived

THE STRATEGY

Prototype A vs. Prototype B

We developed two distinct prototypes to test which model resonated most with users:

Prototype A (The Conventional Approach)

A standard bottom navigation bar prioritizing familiarity and one-tap access to key features.

Prototype B (The All-In-One Hub)

A centralized hub designed to reduce information scattering and consolidate features into a single entry point.

THE SOLUTION

Finding the Meta (Ground)

Our A/B testing revealed a clear trade-off: Prototype A performed well at high-frequency tasks, with users completing Store and Library actions in roughly 6-10 seconds. On the other hand, Prototype B proved better at addressing the issue of hidden functionality by organizing complex tools into a more structured, centralized dashboard.

In our interviews, users consistently preferred Prototype A for its simplicity and familiarity, while appreciating Prototype B's clearer information hierarchy and more intentional grouping of features.

Ultimately, we chose to combine the strongest elements of both layouts:

Prioritize Core Actions

Moved Library, Friends, and Store into persistent bottom navigation to reduce friction and match familiar mobile patterns

Centralize Complex Features

Grouped account-based tools like Steam Families and Settings into a profile hub for clearer organization

Improve Feature Discoverability

Replaced vague labels like “Community” with specific categories like “Forums” to improve discoverability

LESSONS LEARNED

Reflection

This project really put into perspective how important it is to distinguish between mobile and desktop experiences; you can't simply move desktop features into a mobile layout and expect a smooth user journey. It showed my team and me that even the smallest details, like renaming an ambiguous "Community" tag to "Forums" can help users more than we realized.

We saw firsthand that when these details are overlooked, it can easily snowball into a "guessing game" for tasks that should be simple. It taught me that even if an issue may seem minuscule at first glance, it's still important to account for it, because those “small” issues are the exact moments where people decide to give up.
